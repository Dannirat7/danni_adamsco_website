Frequently Asked Questions

Questions are welcome here.

Starting therapy can feel unfamiliar. These answers explain the basics and help families know what to expect.

Have a question not listed here? A care coordinator can help explain services, appointments, privacy, cost, and next steps.

Therapy is evidence-based care provided by a trained mental health professional. It gives children and families a safe place to talk, understand feelings and behavior, learn coping skills, solve problems, and work toward meaningful goals.

Sessions may include talking, listening, play, creative activities, skill practice, problem-solving, and parent or caregiver involvement. The provider will explain the process, privacy rules, and treatment goals at the beginning.

Start by talking with a care coordinator. An intake appointment can help the provider understand your child's strengths, concerns, history, and current needs. The provider will work with you to decide whether therapy or another service is a good fit.

Therapy may help with emotional or behavioral challenges, school or social concerns, major life changes, low self-esteem, anxiety, fears, trauma, grief, anger, attention difficulties, and relationship problems.

Mental health providers protect privacy and confidentiality. They will explain the legal limits, including situations involving immediate safety, suspected abuse or neglect, or a court order. Information is shared with others only when permitted or required.

When a family has health insurance, eligible services are billed to the insurance plan. The program may cover remaining costs or services for families without insurance, based on current program funding and eligibility.

Many sessions are about 50 minutes. Young people often begin weekly or every other week. The overall length of care depends on the child's needs, goals, progress, and family preferences.

A psychiatrist is a medical doctor who evaluates and treats mental, emotional, and behavioral conditions. A psychiatrist can prescribe medication when it is appropriate. Medication is not needed for every child and is often used alongside therapy.

Providers use evidence-based care while considering each child's life experiences, family, culture, school, community, strengths, and goals. Treatment is planned with the youth and caregiver, not simply for them.
